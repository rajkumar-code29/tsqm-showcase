Support the restoration Read her story

Two hundred and fifty turbine steamers were built on this river. She is the last one left.

Not the last in Scotland — the last anywhere. National Historic Ships lists her as nationally pre-eminent. She lies in Glasgow, a mile from where she was launched, out of the water she was built for.

What stands between a hull alongside and a ship under way is steel, boilers and certification. That is all. It is simply expensive.

Her story

Ninety years, one hull, and a gap of nearly forty years in the middle.

  1. 1933

    Launched at Dumbarton

    Built by William Denny and Brothers for Williamson-Buchanan Steamers. A direct-drive turbine steamer for day excursions on the Firth of Clyde.

  2. 1935

    She gives up her name

    Cunard asked for the name for their new Clydebank liner. Her owners agreed and she became Queen Mary II. In exchange Cunard sent a portrait of the Queen, to hang in her forward lounge.

  3. 1939

    Requisitioned

    Withdrawn from excursion service for the duration and put to work as a tender and ferry on the Clyde.

  4. 1957

    Converted to oil

    Her coal-fired boilers replaced. The single funnel she carries today dates from this refit.

  5. 1976

    The name returns

    With the Cunarder retired, she became TS Queen Mary again. The actor Roddy McMillan did the renaming.

  6. 1977

    Withdrawn from service

    Forty-four seasons ended. The last turbine steamer working the Clyde stopped, and nothing replaced her.

  7. 1988

    Moored on the Thames

    Opened in London as a bar and restaurant beside Waterloo Bridge. She stayed there for twenty years.

  8. 2015

    Bought back

    On 21 October the Friends of TS Queen Mary took ownership, having raised some £300,000 in under a year. Robbie Coltrane fronted the campaign.

  9. 2016

    Home to the Clyde

    She came up the firth on 15 May, under tow, forty years after she last sailed it. People stood on the banks to watch.

There are no others

When she goes, that is the end of it.

Sailing again

Back under her own power, carrying passengers on the water she was built for. New steel decks, boiler work, and the certification that lets a 1933 hull carry people in 2027.

A training ship

The first cadet training ship in the UK. Young people learning marine engineering on a working vessel, in a city that used to build one of these a fortnight.

Open to everyone

Alongside in Glasgow when she is not sailing — decks, engine room and all — for school groups, visitors, and anyone who remembers going doon the watter on her.
